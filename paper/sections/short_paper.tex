\section{Introduction}

An option portfolio cannot be scaled or funded like a stock portfolio. The signed premium
paid today is only one cashflow. Short liability, margin, collateral, assignment, and
stress loss are different quantities, and a cheap contract can carry a large normalized
Greek exposure. We therefore begin with the accounting identity, derive option returns
from fixed Greeks, and estimate the complete covariance of those factor exposures and
their residuals. Allocation then becomes a convex, cost-aware problem with cash and hard
survival limits. Whole-contract execution is a separate acceptance step.

The empirical analysis compares three documented specifications. E1 uses a
maximum-Sharpe direction and fails when all VIX-enabled CPCV wealth paths reach zero in
March 2020. R1 replaces that ratio objective with net utility, includes the complete
covariance, admits cash, and applies a 15 percent volatility ceiling. R1.1 keeps those
rules and raises the ceiling to 25 percent.\footnote{The internal codes E1, R1, and R1.1
also appear in artifact filenames and verifier keys.}

Figure~\ref{fig:headline_wealth} reports the retrospective path for the eight-name
plus VIX R1.1 specification. Shading identifies months in which direct integer conversion
violated a hard constraint and the rule selected cash. The implementation does not search
for a substitute risky book.

Mean-variance choice supplies the starting point \citep{markowitz1952portfolio}. The
option return literature connects compensation to option exposure and volatility risk
\citep{coval_shumway2001,bakshi_kapadia2003,bollerslev_tauchen_zhou2009}, while existing
allocation work combines option returns, Greeks, and portfolio choice
\citep{faias_santaclara2017,zhao_palomar2018,malamud2014}. This paper studies funded
option weights, factor-residual cross covariance, constrained net utility, and integer
acceptance within one reproducible allocation rule. The failed E1 specification records
the consequence of evaluating a ratio without the accepted survival rule.
Multiple-testing and combinatorial validation follow
\citet{white2000reality,hansen2005spa,bailey2017pbo,bailey_lopezdeprado2014}.

\section{Funded model and complete covariance}

\subsection{Funded premium weights and NAV}

Let \(C_{i,t}>0\) be the mark of contract \(i\), \(m_i\) its multiplier,
\(N_{i,t}\in\mathbb Z\) the signed number of contracts, and \(W_t>0\) portfolio NAV.
The decision variable is the premium weight
\begin{equation}
  w_{i,t}=\frac{N_{i,t}m_iC_{i,t}}{W_t}.
  \label{eq:premium_weight}
\end{equation}
If \(A_t\) is the marked cash account, then
\begin{equation}
  W_t=A_t+\sum_i N_{i,t}m_iC_{i,t},
  \qquad
  \frac{A_t}{W_t}=1-\one^\top w_t.
  \label{eq:nav_identity}
\end{equation}
For \(r_{i,t+1}=(C_{i,t+1}-C_{i,t})/C_{i,t}\), a self-financing option leg contributes
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta W_{i,t+1}}{W_t}=w_{i,t}r_{i,t+1}.
  \label{eq:nav_return}
\end{equation}
The contract multiplier cancels from the return contribution because it is already in
the weight.

Equation~\eqref{eq:nav_identity} is an accounting identity, not a claim that all marked
cash is free collateral. A long option consumes premium. A short option produces premium
but creates a marked liability whose loss can exceed the initial receipt. Broker margin
is a separate reserve, and capital at risk depends on stress loss, assignment, and
liquidity as well as premium. The optimizer therefore controls gross premium
\(\|w\|_1\), short premium \(\one^\top w^-\), margin, collateral, and tail loss separately.
Any unused admissible capital remains in cash.

\subsection{Second-order option returns}

Over one holding period, a second-order local expansion gives
\begin{equation}
  \Delta C_i
  \simeq \Delta_i\Delta S_i
  +\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_i(\Delta S_i)^2
  +\nu_i\Delta\sigma_i+\Theta_i\Delta t.
  \label{eq:taylor_pnl}
\end{equation}
Writing \(u_i=\Delta S_i/S_i\) and dividing by \(C_i\) produces the loadings
\begin{equation}
  b_i^\Delta=\frac{\Delta_iS_i}{C_i},\qquad
  b_i^\Gamma=\frac{\Gamma_iS_i^2}{2C_i},\qquad
  b_i^\nu=\frac{\nu_i}{C_i}.
  \label{eq:greek_loadings}
\end{equation}
The factor equation centers the quadratic return so that its mean is not counted twice:
\begin{equation}
  r_{i,t+1}=a_{i,t}
  +b_{i,t}^\Delta u_{i,t+1}
  +b_{i,t}^\Gamma\{u_{i,t+1}^2-\E_t[u_{i,t+1}^2]\}
  +b_{i,t}^\nu\Delta\sigma_{i,t+1}
  +\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
  \label{eq:option_factor_return}
\end{equation}
The intercept absorbs theta carry and the conditional mean of the quadratic term. These
loadings are fixed from decision-date marks and Greeks. They are not regression slopes.
The approximation omits higher-order and cross-Greek terms such as vanna and volga; their
realized contribution remains in \(\varepsilon_{i,t+1}\)
\citep{blackscholes1973,merton1973,shreve2004stochasticii}.

For equity options, \(S_i\) is the underlying spot. For VIX options, the price and Greek
anchor is the matched VX futures contract under Black's futures-option model
\citep{black1976commodity}. VIX and VVIX remain state variables, but neither replaces the
traded forward in Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_pnl}. Expiration cashflows use the listed
VRO/SOQ settlement rule.

\subsection{Complete Greek and residual covariance}

Stack the delta, centered-gamma, and vega innovations in \(f_{t+1}\), and let \(B^G_t\)
contain the loadings from Equation~\eqref{eq:greek_loadings}. In vector form,
\begin{equation}
  r_{O,t+1}=\mu_{O,t}+B^G_tf_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{t+1}.
  \label{eq:vector_return}
\end{equation}
The fixed loadings make \(\Cov_t(f_{t+1},\varepsilon_{t+1})\) generally nonzero. An
orthogonal-residual shortcut would therefore discard sample dependence that the Greek
model did not explain.

Define the joint state and its covariance by
\begin{equation}
  Z_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{t+1}\\ \varepsilon_{t+1}\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  S_{Z,t}=\Var_t(Z_{t+1})=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \Omega_t&\Gamma_t\\
    \Gamma_t^\top&\Sigma_{\varepsilon,t}
  \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:joint_covariance}
\end{equation}
The option covariance used by the solver is
\begin{align}
  \Sigma_{O,t}
  &=[B^G_t\ I]S_{Z,t}[B^G_t\ I]^\top \label{eq:complete_covariance}\\
  &=B^G_t\Omega_t(B^G_t)^\top+B^G_t\Gamma_t
    +\Gamma_t^\top(B^G_t)^\top+\Sigma_{\varepsilon,t}. \nonumber
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Positive semidefiniteness]
If \(S_{Z,t}\succeq0\), then \(\Sigma_{O,t}\succeq0\). The result does not require
\(\Gamma_t=0\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any \(x\), set \(y=[B^G_t\ I]^\top x\). Then
\(x^\top\Sigma_{O,t}x=y^\top S_{Z,t}y\ge0\).
\end{proof}

The estimator centers the aligned factor and option observations, forms
\(E=R-F(B^G_t)^\top\), and stacks \(Z=[F\ E]\). It standardizes each joint column,
applies Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage to the standardized covariance, and rescales it
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}. With no shrinkage, Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_covariance}
reconstructs the aligned sample option covariance exactly, including both cross terms.
The regularized estimate is symmetrized, transformed through \([B^G_t\ I]\), and given
one final numerical eigenvalue floor of \(10^{-10}\). Appendix~\ref{app:covariance_proof}
records the estimator algebra.

\section{Estimation and optimization}

\subsection{Structural means and estimation error}

Expected option returns use an auditable structural decomposition:
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde\mu_{i,t}
  =\theta^*_{i,t}
  +b^\Delta_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^S_t
  +b^\Gamma_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{var}_t
  +b^\nu_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{vol}_t
  +\widehat\lambda^{skew}_{i,t}
  +\widehat\lambda^{RV}_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:structural_mean}
\end{equation}
Theta carry, equity exposure, variance and volatility premia, skew or tail insurance, and
within-underlying relative value are retained as separate ledger columns. Skew and tail
terms affect the mean and named stress scenarios; they are not added as covariance
loadings without an identified factor series.

The R1 and R1.1 estimator gives the historical option-return mean zero weight. It
places weight \(0.75\) on Equation~\eqref{eq:structural_mean}, then shrinks the combined
estimate \(75\) percent toward zero:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\mu_t
  =(1-0.75)\{0\cdot\overline r_t+0.75\widetilde\mu_t\}
  =0.1875\,\widetilde\mu_t.
  \label{eq:mean_shrinkage}
\end{equation}
The implementation finally clips a monthly mean only outside \([-0.35,0.35]\). This
construction avoids feeding an unregularized mean for every contract into the optimizer.

\begin{proposition}[Breadth and mean estimation error]
Suppose \(n\) contract-mean errors are sub-Gaussian with scale at most
\(\bar\sigma/\sqrt T\). Then
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[\max_{1\le i\le n}|\widehat\mu_i-\mu_i|\right]
  \le \bar\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2n)}{T}}.
  \label{eq:max_mean_error}
\end{equation}
For every portfolio with \(\|w\|_1\le g\), the corresponding objective perturbation is
at most \(g\|\widehat\mu-\mu\|_\infty\). The raw cross-sectional mean bound is smaller
than a structural bias bound \(\beta\) only when
\begin{equation}
  n<\frac{1}{2}\exp\!\left(\frac{T\beta^2}{2\bar\sigma^2}\right).
  \label{eq:bias_variance_crossover}
\end{equation}
Beyond this crossover, the stated structural bias bound is smaller than the stated raw
mean bound. This comparison does not establish lower total estimation error because it
does not bound the structural estimator's variance. Appendix~\ref{app:mean_error_proof}
gives the proof and the optimizer-regret bound.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The R1 feasible set}

Write \(w=w^+-w^-\), where \(w_i^+=\max(w_i,0)\) and
\(w_i^-=\max(-w_i,0)\). Let \(\ell_{i,t}\) be the point-in-time liquidity cap,
\(a_i\ge0\) the short-margin coefficient, \(\mathcal I_u\) the contracts on underlying
\(u\), and \(\mathcal A_t\) the contracts that cannot be short because assignment is not
admissible. The continuous feasible set includes
\begin{align}
  \|w\|_1&\le1.00, & |\one^\top w|&\le1.00, \label{eq:basic_limits}\\
  \one^\top w^-&\le0.25,
    & |w_i|&\le\min(0.18,\ell_{i,t}), \label{eq:position_limits}\\
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal I_u}|w_i|&\le
    \begin{cases}0.20,&u=\text{VX},\\0.35,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
    & w_i&\ge0\quad(i\in\mathcal A_t), \label{eq:underlying_limits}\\
  |(b^{\beta})^\top w|&\le3.0,
    & |(b^{VIX\nu})^\top w|&\le8.0, \label{eq:exposure_limits}\\
  R^{stress}_tw&\ge-0.20\one,
    & a^\top w^-&\le0.75, \label{eq:stress_margin_limits}\\
  \one^\top w^++a^\top w^-&\le1.00. && \label{eq:funding_limits}
\end{align}
The liquidity cap is computed only from information available at the decision date. The
VX group is labeled \texttt{VX\_FRONT} in code. The beta limit is relative to SPY, and
the vega limit applies to VIX exposure. The stress matrix contains the predefined
scenarios. Equations~\eqref{eq:basic_limits}-\eqref{eq:funding_limits}, together with the
sample CVaR constraint below, define \(\mathcal C_t\).

Every bound is convex and \(0\in\mathcal C_t\). When expected edge does not pay for cost
and risk, the optimizer can decline exposure. A
premium receipt from a short position does not relax the margin or collateral budgets.
The 15 percent annualized R1 volatility ceiling is imposed by the risk-aversion selection
rule rather than inserted as another constraint in the first-stage program.

\subsection{Sample CVaR and asymmetric costs}

Let \(r^{(k)}\) be training scenario \(k\), \(K\) the number of scenarios, and
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t(w)=c_{+,t}^\top w^++c_{-,t}^\top w^-.
  \label{eq:asymmetric_cost}
\end{equation}
The vectors differ because crossing and closing a long position need not cost the same as
opening and covering a short position. The scenario loss includes this predictable cost:
\(L_k(w)=-(r^{(k)})^\top w+\kappa_t(w)\). At \(\alpha=0.95\), the implementation uses the
Rockafellar-Uryasev epigraph \citep{rockafellar_uryasev2000}
\begin{align}
  \widehat{\operatorname{CVaR}}_{\alpha}(w)
  &=\min_{\eta\in\R}\left\{\eta+
  \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)K}\sum_{k=1}^K[L_k(w)-\eta]_+\right\},
  \label{eq:cvar_epigraph}\\
  \widehat{\operatorname{CVaR}}_{0.95}(w)&\le0.10. \nonumber
\end{align}
The complete first-stage allocation is
\begin{equation}
  \max_{w\in\mathcal C_t}
  \left\{\widehat\mu_t^\top w-c_{+,t}^\top w^+
  -c_{-,t}^\top w^- -\frac{\lambda}{2}w^\top\Sigma_{O,t}w\right\}.
  \label{eq:net_utility}
\end{equation}
This is the accepted model for both R1 and the first stage of R1.1.

\begin{proposition}[Convexity and global optimality]
For \(\Sigma_{O,t}\succeq0\) and nonnegative cost vectors, the objective in
Equation~\eqref{eq:net_utility} is concave and \(\mathcal C_t\) is convex. The equivalent
minimization problem is convex, so every computed optimum is global
\citep{boyd_vandenberghe2004}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The cost \(\kappa_t\) and quadratic form are convex. Their negatives are concave. Absolute
value, linear, quadratic-epigraph, and sample-CVaR inequalities define convex sets.
\end{proof}

The optimality condition is most compact in normal-cone form. If \(w^*\) solves
Equation~\eqref{eq:net_utility}, then
\begin{equation}
  0\in-\widehat\mu_t+\partial\kappa_t(w^*)
  +\lambda\Sigma_{O,t}w^*+N_{\mathcal C_t}(w^*).
  \label{eq:kkt_subgradient}
\end{equation}
For one contract, the cost subgradient is \(c_{+,i}\) when \(w_i>0\),
\(-c_{-,i}\) when \(w_i<0\), and the interval \([-c_{-,i},c_{+,i}]\) at zero. A contract
enters only when its marginal expected return clears the appropriate cost wedge after
risk and active constraints are priced. The dual multipliers on CVaR, stress, liquidity,
margin, collateral, and Greek limits are shadow prices for relaxing those budgets. The
full multiplier form appears in Appendix~\ref{app:kkt_proof}.

\begin{proposition}[Existence and uniqueness]
The accepted program has an optimum. If \(\Sigma_{O,t}\succ0\) and \(\lambda>0\), its
portfolio weight is unique.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The set \(\mathcal C_t\) is nonempty because it contains zero. It is closed and bounded
by the gross limit, hence compact. The objective is continuous, so it attains its maximum.
Positive definiteness makes the quadratic penalty strictly convex and the maximization
objective strictly concave, which gives a unique weight even if the CVaR threshold
\(\eta\) is not unique.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:kkt_subgradient} also characterizes cash. The all-cash solution is
optimal exactly when
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\mu_t\in\partial\kappa_t(0)+N_{\mathcal C_t}(0).
  \label{eq:cash_kkt}
\end{equation}
Thus a weak forecast does not require an arbitrary expected-return cutoff. It remains at
zero when the asymmetric trading wedge and the normals of any binding no-short rules
contain the forecast vector.

\subsection{Risk aversion and the volatility ceiling}

Let \(\Phi(w)=\widehat\mu_t^\top w-\kappa_t(w)\),
\(q(w)=w^\top\Sigma_{O,t}w\), and let \(w_\lambda\) be any global solution of
\(\max_{w\in\mathcal C_t}\{\Phi(w)-\lambda q(w)/2\}\).

\begin{proposition}[Optimized variance is nonincreasing]
If \(0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2\), then \(q(w_{\lambda_2})\le q(w_{\lambda_1})\).
\label{prop:risk_monotonicity}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Optimality at \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) gives
\begin{align*}
 \Phi(w_{\lambda_1})-\tfrac{\lambda_1}{2}q(w_{\lambda_1})
 &\ge \Phi(w_{\lambda_2})-\tfrac{\lambda_1}{2}q(w_{\lambda_2}),\\
 \Phi(w_{\lambda_2})-\tfrac{\lambda_2}{2}q(w_{\lambda_2})
 &\ge \Phi(w_{\lambda_1})-\tfrac{\lambda_2}{2}q(w_{\lambda_1}).
\end{align*}
Adding the inequalities yields
\((\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\{q(w_{\lambda_1})-q(w_{\lambda_2})\}\ge0\).
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:risk_monotonicity} justifies a one-dimensional search. The code
solves at \(\lambda=10^{-6}\). If the annualized volatility already meets the ceiling,
that solution is retained. Otherwise it verifies the bracket at \(10^6\) and performs
18 geometric-midpoint steps, equivalent to bisection on \(\log\lambda\). It keeps the
smallest bracketed risk aversion whose solution is at or below 15 percent for R1 and
25 percent for R1.1.

\subsection{R1.1 augmentation and whole contracts}

R1.1 first solves Equation~\eqref{eq:net_utility} with the 25 percent ceiling. Let
\(w^{(1)}\) be this solution and \(s_i=\operatorname{sign}(w_i^{(1)})\). If
\(\|w^{(1)}\|_1<0.50\), the second stage freezes these signs. With
\(d_i=s_i\widehat\mu_i-c_i(s_i)\), it permits augmentation only on
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E=\{i:s_i\ne0,\ d_i>0\},
  \qquad w=s\odot x,\quad x\ge0,\quad x_i=0\ (i\notin\mathcal E).
  \label{eq:r11_directions}
\end{equation}
It then solves the feasibility problem
\begin{align}
  G^*&=\max_x\ \one^\top x \label{eq:r11_feasibility}\\
  \text{subject to}\quad
  s\odot x&\in\mathcal C_t,\qquad x\ge0,\qquad x_i=0\ (i\notin\mathcal E), \nonumber\\
  (s\odot x)^\top\Sigma_{O,t}(s\odot x)
  &\le\left(\frac{0.25}{\sqrt{12}}\right)^2,\qquad d^\top x\ge0, \nonumber\\
  \one^\top x&\le0.50. \nonumber
\end{align}
Only when \(G^*\ge0.50\) does the code impose \(\one^\top x=0.50\) and re-solve net
utility on the retained first-stage directions. Otherwise it keeps \(w^{(1)}\). The target
was never feasible in the historical replay: every universe and arm has a target-hit rate
of 0.00, with mean gross exposure between 0.047 and 0.075.

For account NAV \(W_t\), multiplier 100, and mark \(C_{i,t}\), a continuous weight implies
\begin{equation}
  N_{i,t}=\frac{w_{i,t}W_t}{100C_{i,t}},
  \qquad
  \widehat N_{i,t}=\operatorname{sign}(N_{i,t})
  \left\lfloor|N_{i,t}|\right\rfloor.
  \label{eq:integer_conversion}
\end{equation}
This truncation moves each leg toward cash. The execution code preserves the continuous
signs, including hedge legs whose standalone edge is negative, and rechecks gross, net,
short, contract, underlying, beta, vega, stress, CVaR, margin, collateral, assignment,
liquidity, and volatility limits on the resulting whole-contract book. A feasible direct
conversion trades. An infeasible conversion selects all cash and records the rejected
book's diagnostics. The accepted policy is not a mixed-integer optimizer and does not
search for a substitute integer portfolio.

\subsection{Decision-time algorithm}

The monthly decision follows one fixed sequence.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Form the eligible contract set from decision-date marks, Greeks, maturity,
  moneyness, volume, open interest, quote quality, assignment status, and liquidity caps.
  No later observation can alter this set.
  \item On the trailing training window, compute the structural mean in
  Equation~\eqref{eq:mean_shrinkage}, factor panel, Greek residuals, and regularized joint
  covariance in Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_covariance}.
  \item Build direction-specific costs and the training-scenario losses. Solve
  Equation~\eqref{eq:net_utility} at the risk-aversion endpoints, then use the 18-step
  logarithmic search when the lower endpoint exceeds the policy's volatility ceiling.
  \item For R1.1 only, test Equation~\eqref{eq:r11_feasibility}. The first-stage book is
  retained unless the sign-constrained feasible gross reaches 0.50.
  \item Convert the selected continuous weights through
  Equation~\eqref{eq:integer_conversion}. Recompute every constraint and the volatility
  ceiling on that exact whole-contract book. Trade it if it passes; otherwise record cash.
  \item Realize the next holding-period return under the stated cost and settlement rules,
  write the decision and diagnostics to the ledger, and advance the training endpoint.
\end{enumerate}
Estimation, optimization, and execution acceptance are separate steps. This prevents an
integer repair, a later quote, or a model-price substitution from changing the portfolio
that the continuous program selected.

R1 replaces the E1 objective and acceptance rule. Its covariance
retains factor-residual cross terms, and predictable costs enter utility. Cash is
admissible, offsetting split-leg gross is removed, and the full survival set is checked
before a whole-contract position is accepted.

\subsection{25 percent volatility-ceiling specification (R1.1)}

R1.1 raises the predicted-volatility ceiling to 25 percent. It
pursues a 50 percent gross target only along positive-edge directions that the cost-aware
first stage selects. It never forces that target through an infeasible book. The
continuous target converts directly to whole contracts by truncating toward cash. A
feasible conversion trades. An infeasible conversion abstains.

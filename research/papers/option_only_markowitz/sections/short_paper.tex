\section{Research question and scope}\label{sec:short_intro}
The paper asks whether an option-only allocator can combine premium accounting, Greeks,
expiry, margin, and displayed liquidity into a portfolio procedure that survives realistic
frictions. The evidence available for answering that question is entirely retrospective.
It is therefore development evidence, not a confirmatory holdout or a live track record.

The repaired specification, R1, makes three changes to the earlier E1 development model.
First, it retains the generally nonzero covariance between Greek factors and Greek
residuals. Second, it replaces maximum Sharpe with cost-aware mean--variance utility and
allows the optimizer to hold cash. Third, survival, collateral, margin, and integer
feasibility are hard gates: an absorbed-zero path cannot be rescued by a positive
arithmetic Sharpe ratio. R1 uses only delta, centered squared-return, and implied-volatility
factors in covariance. Skew and tail information enters expected returns and stress tests,
not an exposure matrix that the code does not estimate.

The mathematical ingredients are standard or direct adaptations: Taylor Greek maps,
covariance propagation, mean--variance utility, CVaR, and convex constraints. We do not
claim a new portfolio theorem. The potential contribution is operational and empirical:
putting those ingredients into one auditable, point-in-time option workflow and reporting
where that workflow fails. The earlier E1 results are retained below as legacy development
evidence because they document the research path; they are not the repaired headline model.

Capacity remains a binding finding in the local data. The tested contracts do not support
mechanical scaling from roughly \(\$1\) million to \(\$5\) million NAV. Multi-exchange
routing could change that conclusion, but the present data contain neither routed orders
nor fills, so this paper does not claim that routing solves the constraint.

\subsection{Relation to the literature}

We adapt mean--variance portfolio choice \citep{markowitz1952portfolio,sharpe1966} to
premium-funded option cashflows. Prior option-portfolio work already studies optimized
option allocations, Greek-based risk maps, transaction costs, and mean--variance structure
\citep{faias_santaclara2017,zhao_palomar2018,malamud2014,liang_zhang_li2008}.
Gross-exposure regularization and robust conic portfolio choice also have established
foundations \citep{fan_zhang_yu2012,goldfarb_iyengar2003}. The breadth and estimation-error
mechanics follow the familiar tradeoff between choice-set size and input quality
\citep{grinold_kahn2000,demiguel_garlappi_uppal2009}.
The expected-return channels come from the empirical option-return literature:
expected option returns and their signs
\citep{coval_shumway2001}, delta-hedged gains and the volatility risk premium
\citep{bakshi_kapadia2003}, variance risk premia \citep{carr_wu2009,
bollerslev_tauchen_zhou2009}, index option portfolio evidence
\citep{driessen_maenhout2007,broadie_chernov_johannes2009}, direct option-portfolio
optimization \citep{faias_santaclara2017}, and the embedded-leverage demand that makes
cheap convexity systematically expensive \citep{frazzini_pedersen2022}. The validation
protocol draws on the backtest-overfitting and multiple-testing literature
\citep{white2000reality,hansen2005spa,bailey2017pbo,bailey_lopezdeprado2014,
lopezdeprado2018}. The warning that option-like payoffs can manufacture attractive
Sharpe ratios while retaining rare crash losses is central here
\citep{goetzmann_ingersoll_spiegel_welch2007}.

Relative to this literature, the marginal contribution sought here is the integrated
implementation: contract-level premium accounting, a complete Greek/residual joint
covariance, pre-trade costs and liquidity caps, explicit collateral and margin, exact
versus proxied spread accounting, and VIX settlement handling. None is novel in isolation.
The paper supplies self-contained derivations to document the implementation, not to claim
priority for standard mathematics.

\section{Theory: option allocation as funded cashflows}\label{sec:short_theory}

The object of the paper is an option book, not a transformed equity book. An option
contract \(i\) at decision date \(t\) has a mark \(C_{i,t}\), multiplier \(m_i\), payoff
rule, settlement convention, Greeks, displayed liquidity, and a forecast of conditional
option compensation. A portfolio weight \(w_i\) is the premium notional committed to that
contract divided by NAV. These are \emph{premium weights}. A long option with
\(w_i=0.03\) can lose at most three percent of NAV before transaction costs and financing;
a short option with \(w_i=-0.03\) receives three percent of NAV in premium but carries a
future mark-to-market and settlement liability. Cash is the collateral numeraire.

This distinction is the main mathematical reason the option-only problem differs from
ordinary stock Markowitz. Stock weights usually satisfy a unit-budget equation. Option
premium weights do not. Residual cash, margin collateral, and unpaid short-option losses
are separate accounting objects. The correct bridge from theory to implementation is
therefore to write option cashflows explicitly, build a Greek-induced covariance for those
cashflows, and then constrain the resulting allocation by premium, stress, margin, and
liquidity budgets.

\subsection{Why ordinary stock Markowitz is insufficient}

Ordinary Markowitz \citep{markowitz1952portfolio} starts from returns on assets that can
be held with weights summing to one. The budget equation does much of the economic work:
it fixes scale, makes positive and negative weights interpretable as capital allocation
and borrowing, and leaves the covariance matrix as the main risk object. Listed options
break that simplification.
Premium paid is not the same as capital at risk for a short option; margin required is not
the same as premium received; and a low-premium option can carry large delta, gamma, vega,
and gap exposure. A variance penalty alone cannot prevent the optimizer from selecting
positions that are cheap by premium but expensive by stress loss or market depth.

The right mathematical analogue is therefore not a simple replacement of stock returns by
option returns. It is a funded cashflow problem. Premium weights determine the mark-to-
market contribution. Collateral and margin constraints determine whether the position can
be carried. Greek and stress constraints determine whether local variance misses a bad
state. Liquidity caps determine whether the target can be traded at the account size. The
portfolio problem becomes a constrained allocation over option cashflow claims, with cash
and collateral as the reference asset.

This also explains why the paper keeps a stock Markowitz baseline. If the option-only
framework cannot beat ordinary underlying Markowitz after costs and caps, the option
structure has not earned its complexity. The stock baseline is not expected to match the
option payoff distribution; it is a useful economic hurdle because it asks whether the
same underlyings could have been traded more simply. The capped naive option baseline
plays the role that \(1/N\) plays in the stock literature
\citep{demiguel_garlappi_uppal2009}: if simple diversification matches the optimizer
after the same costs and caps, the estimation machinery has not paid for itself.

\subsection{Premium accounting}

Let \(W_t\) be portfolio NAV at the rebalance date and let \(N_{i,t}\) be the signed
number of contracts held in option \(i\). With multiplier \(m_i\), define
\[
  w_{i,t}=\frac{N_{i,t}m_iC_{i,t}}{W_t}.
\]
For equity options \(m_i=100\). For VIX options the multiplier convention is handled in
the same way through \(m_i\). The one-period option mark return is
\[
  r_{i,t+1}=\frac{C_{i,t+1}-C_{i,t}}{C_{i,t}},
  \qquad
  \frac{\Delta W_{i,t+1}}{W_t}=w_{i,t}r_{i,t+1}.
\]
The multiplier cancels because the weight is already premium-normalized. This is why a
cheap out-of-the-money option can have enormous return exposure but still receive a small
premium weight. It is also why gross premium, contract concentration, Greek exposure, and
stress loss cannot be replaced by a single variance constraint.

\begin{proposition}[NAV accounting identity]\label{prop:short_nav}
Let \(w_t\in\R^n\) be the option premium-weight vector, \(r_{t+1}\) the option mark-return
vector, and \(r_{f,t}\) the one-period return on residual cash
\(W_t(1-\one^\top w_t)\). Ignoring non-option collateral adjustments for notation,
\[
  \frac{W_{t+1}}{W_t}-1
  =w_t^\top r_{t+1}+\bigl(1-\one^\top w_t\bigr)r_{f,t},
  \qquad
  \frac{W_{t+1}}{W_t}-1-r_{f,t}
  =w_t^\top(r_{t+1}-r_{f,t}\one).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The option book is worth
\(\sum_iN_{i,t}m_iC_{i,t+1}=W_t\sum_iw_{i,t}(1+r_{i,t+1})\). The residual cash account is
worth \(W_t(1-\one^\top w_t)(1+r_{f,t})\). Adding both accounts, dividing by \(W_t\), and
subtracting one gives the identity.
\end{proof}

The empirical tables in this paper use a zero-cash benchmark for comparability across
strategy variants. Proposition~\ref{prop:short_nav} is the conversion rule if a reader
wants excess-of-cash returns instead. Positive collateral yield would add income to
cash-rich long-option books and raise the hurdle for excess Sharpe ratios. The comparison
between the four option variants and the two baselines remains internally consistent
because the same convention is applied throughout.

Two details matter for implementation. First, a long option cannot lose more than its
premium, but a short option can lose far more than the premium it collected. The premium
weight \(w_i\) is therefore an accounting weight, not a complete risk measure. Second,
every R1 result in this paper is reported for a whole-contract book. The net-utility
solver returns continuous premium weights, but each weight is converted to a signed
integer number of contracts before the book is scored, so the Sharpe ratios, Sortino
ratios, cost figures, and ledgers below all describe integer positions at the standard
\(m_i\) multiplier. Remark~\ref{rem:short_rounding} states the conversion rule.

\begin{remark}[Whole-contract rounding]\label{rem:short_rounding}
A premium weight \(w_i\) implies a continuous contract count \(N_i=w_iW_t/(m_iC_{i,t})\).
The R1 book truncates \(N_i\) toward zero so that integerization cannot deliberately add
risk. It then checks every CVaR, stress, margin, collateral, Greek, and liquidity constraint.
If the integer book is infeasible, the decision fails closed to cash. The legacy E1 book
rounded to the nearest integer and, wherever a per-contract
liquidity cap bound, stepped the magnitude down by one contract so that the realized weight
\(\widehat w_i=N_im_iC_{i,t}/W_t\) never exceeds the cap. All four option books and both
capped naive baselines in the legacy tables are scored on those realized integer weights.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Greek-induced covariance}

The option return map comes from a local state expansion. For an equity option on
underlying \(u\), write \(\Delta S_u=S_{u,t+1}-S_{u,t}\) and
\(R_u=\Delta S_u/S_{u,t}\). The mark change is decomposed as
\[
  \Delta C_i
  =
  \Delta^{BS}_{i,t}\Delta S_u
  +\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{i,t}(\Delta S_u)^2
  +\nu_{i,t}\Delta\sigma_{i,t}
  +\Theta_{i,t}\Delta t
  +\eta_{i,t+1}.
\]
The remainder \(\eta_{i,t+1}\) is defined to make this an identity at the rebalance
horizon. It absorbs vanna, volga, smile movement, early-exercise premium changes, stale
marks, and all higher-order terms not separately modeled. The paper does not require
Black--Scholes--Merton to be the true global pricing model for American single-name
options. It uses the weaker local fact that option marks can be projected onto spot,
curvature, volatility, and time channels \citep{blackscholes1973,merton1973}.

Dividing by \(C_{i,t}\) gives premium-normalized state exposure,
\[
  r_{i,t+1}
  =
  b^\Delta_{i,t}R_{u,t+1}
  +b^\Gamma_{i,t}R_{u,t+1}^2
  +b^\nu_{i,t}\Delta\sigma_{i,t}
  +\theta^\ast_{i,t}
  +\epsilon_{i,t+1},
\]
where
\[
  b^\Delta_{i,t}=\frac{\Delta^{BS}_{i,t}S_{u,t}}{C_{i,t}},\quad
  b^\Gamma_{i,t}=\frac{\Gamma_{i,t}S_{u,t}^2}{2C_{i,t}},\quad
  b^\nu_{i,t}=\frac{\nu_{i,t}}{C_{i,t}},\quad
  \theta^\ast_{i,t}=\frac{\Theta_{i,t}\Delta t}{C_{i,t}}.
\]
These coefficients are option-return exposures, not dollar Greeks. Their magnitudes can
be large because the option premium can be small. That feature is economically correct:
cheap convexity is precisely where leverage, spread costs, and liquidity scarcity enter.

For VIX options the traded anchor is not equity spot but the matched VX futures curve.
Using Black's futures-option logic \citep{black1976commodity}, a VIX option \(j\) with
matched forward \(F^{VX}_{T_j,t}\) enters through
\[
  r_{j,t+1}
  =
  b^{\Delta,VX}_{j,t}R^{VX}_{T_j,t+1}
  +b^{\Gamma,VX}_{j,t}(R^{VX}_{T_j,t+1})^2
  +b^{\nu,VIX}_{j,t}\Delta\sigma^{VIX}_{j,t}
  +\theta^\ast_{j,t}
  +\epsilon_{j,t+1}.
\]
VIX, VVIX, and term-structure indices are useful state variables, but VX futures and VIX
options are the traded volatility instruments. Exact VRO/SOQ settlement is required
before VIX option expiry P\&L can be promoted from settlement proxy to listed-settlement
evidence.

Stacking all contracts gives the conditional factor model
\[
  r_{O,t+1}=\mu_{O,t}+B^G_t f_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{t+1}.
\]
The implemented factor vector contains only underlying returns, centered squared returns,
and implied-volatility changes. The fixed matrix \(B^G_t\) contains the corresponding
premium-normalized delta, gamma, and vega loadings. Define
\[
 \Omega_t=\Var_t(f_{t+1}),\qquad
 \Gamma_t=\Cov_t(f_{t+1},\varepsilon_{t+1}),\qquad
 \Sigma_{\varepsilon,t}=\Var_t(\varepsilon_{t+1}).
\]
Because \(B^G_t\) is fixed from Greeks rather than estimated as a regression coefficient,
there is no reason for \(\Gamma_t\) to vanish.

\begin{proposition}[Covariance identity]\label{prop:short_covariance}
For any fixed Greek loading matrix \(B^G_t\),
\[
  \Var_t(r_{O,t+1})
  =B^G_t\Omega_t(B^G_t)^\top
   +B^G_t\Gamma_t+\Gamma_t^\top(B^G_t)^\top
   +\Sigma_{\varepsilon,t}.
\]
Equivalently, with \(A_t=[B^G_t\ I]\) and
\(
S_t=\Var_t([f_{t+1}^\top,\varepsilon_{t+1}^\top]^\top),
\)
the option covariance is \(A_tS_tA_t^\top\succeq0\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expand the variance of \(B^G_tf_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{t+1}\). The two cross-covariance
terms remain. The block form follows by multiplication, and positive semidefiniteness
follows because a linear image of a covariance matrix is positive semidefinite.
\end{proof}

If instead \(B_t=\Cov_t(r_{O,t+1},f_{t+1})\Omega_t^{-1}\), the residual is an OLS
projection residual and \(\Gamma_t=0\). That familiar projection formula is a corollary,
not the definition used by R1. Empirically, R1 centers aligned training returns and factors,
forms \(\varepsilon=r-B^Gf\), estimates the regularized joint covariance of
\([f,\varepsilon]\) on the correlation scale, and transforms it by \([B^G\ I]\). With
regularization disabled, the construction reproduces the aligned sample option covariance
to numerical precision. Only a final numerical eigenvalue floor is applied after the joint
transformation.

\subsection{State factors and residual risk}

The factor map has a deliberately narrow interpretation. Delta exposure routes option
returns into underlying or VX-forward returns. Gamma routes them into centered quadratic
movement. Vega routes them into implied-volatility changes. Skew and tail variables proxy
where crash insurance is being repriced in the mean and stress models; they are not claimed
as covariance loadings.

The residual term is where the model acknowledges its limits. If option marks were perfectly spanned
by spot, variance, volatility, skew, and VIX-forward factors, \(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,t}\)
would be zero. It is not. Residual risk captures early exercise, discrete dividends,
surface deformations, quote noise, strike/maturity mismatch, and the fact that a monthly
rebalance is not an infinitesimal Ito step. The model can use Greeks without pretending
that the Greek map is complete.

The diagonal residual choice in legacy E1 should be read in this spirit. It is not a claim that
residual risks are truly independent. It is a regularization decision. In a broad option
universe, a dense residual covariance matrix estimated from a short and unbalanced panel
can create false diversification and unstable inverse-covariance directions. Diagonal
residual covariance keeps contract-specific unexplained risk while refusing to let noisy
off-diagonal residual estimates dominate the optimizer. R1 supersedes that approximation
with the regularized joint covariance and keeps the observed factor/residual dependence.

\subsection{Expected option compensation}

Greek covariance alone cannot create a Sharpe ratio. The optimizer needs a conditional
mean vector. The economic decomposition is
\[
\widehat\mu_{i,t}
=b^\Delta_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^S_t
+b^\Gamma_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{var}_t
+b^\nu_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{vol}_t
+b^{skew}_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{skew}_t
+b^{tail}_{i,t}\widehat\lambda^{tail}_t
+\theta^\ast_{i,t}.
\]
Delta loads on equity compensation. Gamma and theta jointly encode the realized-versus-
implied variance wedge. Vega loads on volatility-risk compensation. Skew and tail terms
represent compensation for crash insurance and convex volatility exposure, which the
option-return literature documents in multiple forms
\citep{coval_shumway2001,bakshi_kapadia2003,carr_wu2009,bollerslev_tauchen_zhou2009}.

The implemented signal is simpler than the general display. It combines
carry, a damped delta channel, a vega-routed implied-minus-realized volatility wedge, and
skew/tail characteristics. Cheap convexity is treated as systematically expensive rather
than anomalous, consistent with the embedded-leverage evidence of
\citet{frazzini_pedersen2022}. The legacy E1 version then removes the raw
historical option mean entirely and shrinks the structural forecast toward zero by
\(0.75\). This is not an aesthetic choice. It is the central empirical lesson of the
breadth experiments, and it is the option-panel version of the classical breadth-versus-
input-quality tension \citep{grinold_kahn2000}: the historical mean is useful when the
option universe is small and the training ratio \(T/n\) is healthy, but it becomes a
ranking-noise generator when the optimizer can choose among many correlated contracts.

\begin{proposition}[Historical mean error and breadth]\label{prop:short_mean_error}
Let the training window contain \(T\) monthly observations of \(n\) contracts, and let
the sample-mean errors \(e_i=\widehat\mu^{\,\mathrm{hist}}_i-\mu_i\) be mean zero and
sub-Gaussian with variance proxies \(\sigma_i^2/T\), \(\bar\sigma=\max_i\sigma_i\). Then
\[
  \E\Big[\max_i |e_i|\Big]\;\le\;\bar\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2n)}{T}},
\]
and for any feasible set \(\mathcal C\subseteq\{w:\|w\|_1\le g\}\) the plug-in optimizer's
expected objective inflation obeys
\(\E\big[\sup_{w\in\mathcal C}e^\top w\big]\le g\,\bar\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2n)/T}\).
The allocation chosen under the noisy mean therefore sacrifices at most
\(2g\,\bar\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2n)/T}\) of true objective value in expectation, and this
worst-case loss grows with \(n\) at fixed \(T\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Bias--variance crossover]\label{cor:short_crossover}
Suppose the structural forecast has a deterministic specification bias bounded by
\(\beta=\|\mu^{\mathrm{struct}}-\mu\|_\infty\) that does not grow with \(n\). Comparing
worst-case objective losses, the historical mean is preferred only when
\(\bar\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2n)/T}<\beta\), that is, when
\(n<\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\big(T\beta^2/2\bar\sigma^2\big)\). With \(T\) fixed, a historical
option mean can therefore help at small \(n\) and hurt at broad \(n\).
\end{corollary}

Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:mean_error}. The sub-Gaussian model is a stylization:
option panels add correlation, missingness, wide tails, and changing contract
composition, all of which raise the effective error at a given \((n,T)\) rather than
lower it. The bound therefore understates the practical penalty of ranking many nearly
substitutable contracts by noisy estimated means.

The crossover is visible in the artifacts. At eight names, dropping the historical mean
hurts the no-VIX gross Sharpe, moving it from \(0.842\) to \(0.725\). In the broad universe,
keeping the historical mean is the dominant source of optimizer failure: structural-only
forecasts and stronger shrinkage are what allow the \(56\)-name and \(57\)-name books to
become viable portfolios. The theory does not say the historical mean is always
bad. It says the mean estimator must respect the ratio between sample length and choice
set.

\begin{remark}[Shrinkage to zero as a posterior mean]\label{rem:short_bayes}
The \(0.75\) shrink-to-zero factor has a standard Bayesian reading. If the true premium
satisfies \(\mu_i\sim N(0,\tau^2)\) and the structural forecast is unbiased with noise
\(\widehat\mu^{\,\mathrm{struct}}_i\mid\mu_i\sim N(\mu_i,s^2)\), the posterior mean is
\((1-\kappa)\,\widehat\mu^{\,\mathrm{struct}}_i\) with
\(\kappa=s^2/(s^2+\tau^2)\). E1's \(\kappa=0.75\) corresponds to a prior signal-to-noise
ratio \(\tau^2/s^2=1/3\): the forecast is treated as three parts noise to one part
signal. Because the structural blend itself carries a weight of \(0.75\), the effective
multiplier on the raw structural signal is \((1-0.75)\times 0.75=0.1875\). The same
regularize-toward-a-simple-target logic is applied to the covariance
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}. Both parameters were fixed in the earlier diagnostic phase and
locked before the validation runs; neither is re-tuned inside folds.
\end{remark}

\subsection{From economic premia to the E1 signal}

The structural mean used by E1 can be summarized as four channels. The carry channel is
\(\theta^\ast_{i,t}\), the premium-normalized theta contribution under unchanged state
variables. The delta channel applies a damped forecast of the underlying's training-window
return to \(b^\Delta_{i,t}\). The volatility channel routes the implied-minus-realized
gap through vega rather than double-counting it through both gamma and theta. The
skew/tail channel gives insurance-like wings a negative expected return for long
positions and a positive expected return for short positions, subject to stress and
liquidity caps.

This construction favors stable economic signs over flexible statistical fit. It does not
estimate a separate mean for every contract from that contract's past returns. R1 retains
the structural-only, 0.75 shrinkage rule identified during E1 development, but does not
describe that choice as prospectively selected.

The no-free-lunch constraint is that structural premia must be paid for in risk budgets.
Shorting skew or volatility can earn compensation precisely because those positions lose
in crash and volatility-spike states. The optimizer is therefore never allowed to view the
mean in isolation. A contract with attractive expected carry can still be excluded by
stress loss, vega exposure, beta exposure, margin, concentration, or liquidity.

\subsection{Robust conic allocation}

Given \(\widehat\mu_t\) and \(\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}\), the headline allocation
is
\[
  \max_{w\in\mathcal C_t}
  \widehat\mu_t^\top w
  -\frac{\gamma}{2}w^\top\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}w
  -(c_t^+)^\top w^+-(c_t^-)^\top w^-,
  \tag{P}
\]
where \(w^+=\max(w,0)\) and \(w^-=\max(-w,0)\). The asymmetric cost vectors include
spread, explicit fees, slippage, borrow, and margin funding; charging these costs inside
the objective rather than after it follows the cost-aware trading literature
\citep{garleanu_pedersen2013}. The feasible set \(\mathcal C_t\) imposes premium, Greek,
beta/VIX, stress-loss, margin/collateral, concentration, and liquidity constraints. The
R1 implementation solves (P) directly. Gross premium is an upper bound, not an equality,
so unattractive opportunities produce cash. Capacity enters through hard point-in-time
caps rather than a reward for consuming volume.

The policy fixes annualized predicted volatility at no more than 15 percent by choosing
\(\gamma>0\) through deterministic training-only bisection. Monthly 95 percent CVaR is at
most 10 percent NAV, named stress loss is at most 20 percent, short margin is at most
75 percent, and long premium plus short margin is at most 100 percent NAV. Equity-option
shorts flagged for assignment risk are prohibited rather than assigned an ex-post penalty.
Because \(\gamma>0\) and the numerical covariance floor makes
\(\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}\succ0\), the utility objective is strictly concave and selects a
unique scale.

The legacy unconstrained maximum-Sharpe geometry remains a useful warning. If
\(\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}\succ0\) and \(\widehat\mu_t\neq0\), the maximum-Sharpe direction is
proportional to \(\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}^{-1}\widehat\mu_t\). Unlike a stock-only problem,
there is no required \(\one^\top w=1\) budget that bends the efficient frontier into the
usual hyperbola.

\begin{proposition}[Option-only frontier is a ray]\label{prop:short_ray}
Let \(\Sigma\succ0\) and \(\mu\neq0\). For a target option mean \(m>0\), the
minimum-variance premium-weight vector in the unconstrained problem is
\[
  w^\ast(m)=\frac{m\,\Sigma^{-1}\mu}{\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu},
  \qquad
  \sigma(m)=\frac{m}{\sqrt{\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu}}.
\]
Thus the unconstrained efficient frontier in \((\sigma,m)\) space is a ray through the
origin. This describes why maximum Sharpe cannot choose book size; it is not the R1
allocation rule.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Lagrangian for \(\min_w w^\top\Sigma w\) subject to \(\mu^\top w=m\) yields
\(2\Sigma w=\lambda\mu\). The constraint pins
\(\lambda/2=m/(\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu)\). Substitution gives the displayed variance.
\end{proof}

The most important operational constraint in this paper is the net liquidity cap
\[
  |w_i|\le \bar w_{i,t},
  \qquad
  \bar w_{i,t}=\min\!\left\{0.18,\,
  \frac{X\,\widehat V^{train}_{i,t}\,M_{i,t}\,100}{\mathrm{NAV}}\right\}.
\]
Here \(X=0.05\), \(M_{i,t}\) is the option mark, and \(\widehat V^{train}_{i,t}\) is a
training-window volume statistic. The cap is point-in-time because it uses only
train-window volume. It is net because it binds the economic position \(w_i\), not the
positive and negative solver legs separately.

\begin{proposition}[Net caps and representation invariance]\label{prop:short_net_caps}
A cap on \(|w_i|\) is invariant to algebraic representation of a contract position. A cap
on separate long and short legs is not representation-invariant and can create pseudo-cash
artifacts.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The economic position is \(w_i=w_i^+-w_i^-\). The bound \(|w_i|\le \bar w_i\) depends only
on that net exposure. Separate bounds on \(w_i^+\) and \(w_i^-\) allow both legs to be
positive while the net position is small, so a solver can consume displayed capacity in
offsetting long/short pairs without taking the intended exposure.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Costs and tighter feasible sets]\label{prop:short_costs}
If costs rise componentwise or \(\mathcal C_t\) is tightened, the optimal value of
\((P)\) weakly decreases. With \(\gamma>0\) and
\(\widehat\Sigma_{O,t}\succ0\), (P) has at most one optimizer. Zero is feasible, so the
program may select an all-cash risky book.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any fixed feasible \(w\), increasing either cost vector weakly lowers the objective,
and maximizing over a subset cannot improve the supremum. The negative quadratic is
strictly concave under positive definiteness and \(\gamma>0\), while the remaining terms
are concave, giving uniqueness. Feasibility of zero follows from the upper-bound form of
every budget.
\end{proof}

These propositions delimit what the empirical sections can and cannot show. The paper is
not trying to show that any option signal can be made to work with enough leverage. It
asks whether a regularized, cap-constrained option allocation survives when the
market frictions that matter for small-account trading are imposed before performance is
reported.

\subsection{Explicit constraint set}

A useful way to read the feasible set is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal C_t=\{w\in\R^n:\quad
&\|w\|_1\le g,\qquad
\sum_{i:w_i<0}|w_i|\le s,\qquad
|w_i|\le \bar w_{i,t}\ \forall i,\\
&\sum_{i:u(i)=u}|w_i|\le g_u\ \forall u,\qquad
|a_{k,t}^\top w|\le b_k\ \forall k,\\
&\ell_{j,t}^\top w\ge -m_j\ \forall j,\qquad
\operatorname{CVaR}_{0.95}(-R^{train}_tw+c_t(w))\le0.10,\\
&\mathrm{ShortMargin}_t(w^-)\le0.75,\qquad
\one^\top w^++\mathrm{ShortMargin}_t(w^-)\le1,\\
&w_i\ge0\quad\text{for assignment-flagged equity options}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first line controls gross premium, short premium, and contract-level liquidity. The
second controls underlying concentration and Greek/beta/VIX budgets. The third controls
scenario stress loss, CVaR, collateral, margin, and assignment eligibility. The vectors \(a_{k,t}\) include
NAV-normalized delta, beta, gamma, equity vega, VIX vega, and other exposure rows. The
stress rows \(\ell_{j,t}\) reprice the book under adverse spot, volatility, and VIX
shocks.

The quadratic risk term is convex in minimization form, and positive/negative parts price
asymmetric costs and margin without imposing a gross equality. Liquidity binds the net
economic variable. R1 therefore has no split-leg pseudo-cash mechanism: it represents one
position vector \(w\), constrains \(\|w\|_1\) from above, and leaves unused capital in cash.
Appendix~\ref{app:technical} records the legacy maximum-Sharpe geometry only to explain why
it was replaced.

\subsection{Algorithmic summary}

The repaired R1 procedure can be written as a short algorithm.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At each monthly decision date, build the eligible option universe and compute
  premium-normalized Greeks, current costs, marks, volume caps, margin coefficients,
  assignment flags, and settlement labels.
  \item Estimate the regularized joint covariance of delta/gamma/vega factors and Greek
  residual returns, including both factor/residual cross terms.
  \item Form the structural expected-return vector from carry, delta, volatility,
  skew/tail, and VIX channels; set historical option mean weight to zero; shrink the mean
  \(75\%\) toward zero.
  \item Solve net utility with the fixed 15 percent volatility policy and hard CVaR,
  stress, margin, collateral, assignment, and liquidity limits.
  \item Truncate contract counts toward zero, recheck every constraint, fail closed to
  cash when necessary, and evaluate only the next holding period.
\end{enumerate}

All rolling refits rebuild
the representative contracts, mean/covariance estimates, and liquidity caps from the
training window. Liquidity caps use training-window volume only. All observations currently
available were seen during research development; these checks are internal validation, not
an untouched holdout.

\section{Empirical application}\label{sec:short_application}

The empirical application turns the clean conic allocation of
Section~\ref{sec:short_theory} into an exchange-realistic book by layering three
data-driven frictions onto the idealized problem. First, every position is priced through
a full transaction-cost stack---spread crossing, fees, slippage, borrow, and margin funding---
built from historical market-hours CBBO quotes rather than a flat
default spread. Second, each contract carries a volume-aware pre-trade liquidity cap that
sizes it to a fixed fraction \(X=0.05\) of its displayed training-window volume, so the
optimizer can only take exposure the market actually showed (the cap \(\bar w_{i,t}\) of
Section~\ref{sec:short_theory}). Third, the continuous premium weights are truncated to whole
option contracts at the standard \(m_i\) multiplier before any performance number is
computed and all constraints are checked again (Remark~\ref{rem:short_rounding}). The theory says which exposures to hold; these
three layers constrain the book to the spreads, depth, and contract granularity that the
historical data actually recorded, and every R1 development result is reported only after all
three are imposed. The subsections below give the four universes, the cost stack and its
spread sourcing, and the validation protocol in turn.

\subsection{Four universes, repaired R1, and legacy E1}

The empirical design uses four implementation universes. \texttt{orig} is the
original eight equity-option names: AAPL, AMZN, GOOGL, JPM, META, MSFT, NVDA, and TSLA.
\texttt{orig+VIX} adds VIX options. \texttt{larger} expands the equity-option universe to
56 names. \texttt{larger+VIX} adds VIX to that broader set. The four variants are not
four different optimizers. R1 applies the same fixed risk policy to each universe.

Legacy E1 used structural-only means, diagonal residual covariance, \(N\)-scaled covariance

covariance and objective, and labels every existing observation retrospective.

The contract construction is representative rather than high-frequency. Each rebalance
date maps the eligible option panel into month-end representative contracts and R1 refits
using only information available by that decision date before holding the book for one period. This choice
makes the backtest auditable: the research question is whether a monthly,
capacity-aware option allocation works after costs, not whether intraday execution timing
can rescue a fragile signal. It also makes the capacity result sharper. If month-end
representative contracts cannot support the desired NAV under a five-percent participation
cap, the strategy should not be promoted by assuming invisible intraday depth.

The two baselines are chosen for interpretability. The first is ordinary Markowitz on the
underlying stocks. It asks whether option structure adds anything beyond an equity
portfolio on the same economic names. The second is a capped naive option book: equal
premium or equal risk after applying the same liquidity caps and costs. It asks whether
the Greek/mean/covariance theory beats a mechanically diversified options sleeve. The
paper's claim requires beating both baselines while remaining deployable under the cap
budget.

The two baselines serve different failure modes. Stock Markowitz is a simplicity
benchmark: if the option book cannot beat a portfolio of underlying shares, the option
engineering may be unnecessary. The capped naive option book is a structure benchmark:
if equal-premium or equal-risk option diversification performs as well as R1, then the
Greek covariance and structural mean are not adding much. The broad no-VIX result fails
this second test in the legacy evidence because its E1 Sharpe is essentially tied with capped naive options.

\subsection{Cost stack and spread sourcing}

R1 prices the predictable portion of spread crossing, fees, slippage, borrow, and margin
funding inside optimization and uses capacity as a hard cap. Legacy E1 deducted the full
cost stack only after allocation. The most
consequential design decision in that stack is spread sourcing. A flat \(10\%\) default
spread is not a realistic representation of liquid large-cap options and materially
deflates large parts of the option universe. The corrected stack uses exact historical
market-hours panel CBBO where available and an inferred CBBO proxy only where matched
broad historical quotes are missing.

The full-cost return for a book is therefore not simply gross return minus one spread
number. Costs enter through several channels. Spread and fee costs scale with the target
option premium traded. Slippage is a further execution allowance. Borrow and margin drag
represent the cost of carrying short or collateral-intensive exposure. Assignment penalty
is a fail-closed proxy for short American-option exercise risk. Capacity/impact costs
penalize positions that try to consume too much displayed volume. The corrected headline
results use these costs together; no row is promoted from a gross-only statistic.

Spread sourcing shapes what the results can support because OPRA is the consolidated U.S.
options quote and last-sale system,\footnote{Options Price Reporting Authority:
\url{https://www.opraplan.com/}.} and Cboe sells historical option NBBO snapshots with
size, including 15:45 snapshots intended to be more representative than end-of-day
liquidity.\footnote{Cboe DataShop Option EOD Summary:
\url{https://datashop.cboe.com/option-eod-summary}.} Matched broad historical
market-hours NBBO/CBBO is generally not free. Therefore this paper separates exact
evidence from calibrated execution sensitivity. The baseline eight equity-option rows
use exact historical panel CBBO. VIX rows and missing broad-name rows use a point-in-time
inferred CBBO proxy calibrated from the liquid CBBO surface. Proxy rows are not final
execution truth.

the broad exercise: the proxy is point-in-time and calibrated from observed liquid-option
CBBO. It does, however, prevent a final execution claim. The broad result is best read as:
under realistic liquid-option spread assumptions, the optimizer survives; under exact
historical broad NBBO/CBBO, this still needs to be confirmed.

\subsection{Validation protocol}

R1 is a monthly walk-forward development exercise. For each return month, the information
set, representative contracts, costs, conditional means, joint covariance, margin inputs,
and volume caps are reconstructed from observations available no later than the decision
date. The resulting integer book is evaluated only in the next period. The four variants
use \(\$1\) million NAV and \(X=0.05\) participation. Cash is permitted, so capacity
limits reduce deployment instead of inducing pseudo-cash or algebraic infeasibility.

The legacy robustness layer uses 12 chronological groups, 66 CPCV splits run in both a
liquid-era (post-2018) design and a claim-window design restricted to the post-2020
evaluation months (Section~\ref{sec:short_robustness}), 78 total CV splits in
the PBO summary \citep{bailey2017pbo,lopezdeprado2018}, one-month purge/embargo, Monte
Carlo resampled histories built from circular-block bootstrap draws
\citep{politis_romano1994}, Monte Carlo refit stability, repriced synthetic option
universes with a historical-cost overlay, circular-block bootstrap path simulations,
GARCH/EGARCH-style volatility-path diagnostics \citep{nelson1991} with fallback when the
EGARCH sample is too short, drawdown breach simulations, multiple-testing/reality-check
inference, and rolling 36-month OOS refits. These checks are designed to attack the
result from multiple angles. Because E1 had already been selected using the post-2020
window, these diagnostics do not create a confirmatory holdout.

The purge and embargo rule is simple: one month is dropped around fold
boundaries so option labels whose payoff windows overlap the test block do not leak into
training. Lemma~\ref{lem:purge_gap} in the appendix records why one month is sufficient:
the tested contracts' decision-to-expiry spans are at most about \(44\) calendar days,
while the verifier-measured minimum calendar gap between any usable training month and
the tested months---taken on both sides of each test block, since combinatorial folds
also train on later months---is \(59\) days across all folds. The CPCV exercise is distributional,
not tradable, because some training sets can include future calendar months relative to a
tested block. Rolling OOS is closer to a trading protocol because it refits through time.
Monte Carlo refit stability asks whether the procedure selects similar risk-adjusted
books under alternate training histories. Repriced synthetic paths ask whether the
strategy depends too much on one realized option surface path. Each diagnostic attacks a
different source of false confidence.

\subsection{Performance metrics and deployability}

The primary R1 outcomes are terminal wealth, annualized geometric growth, maximum drawdown,
worst month, 95 percent expected shortfall, ruin, margin and collateral breaches, integer
feasibility, and capacity. Sharpe and Sortino are secondary descriptors. With monthly
returns \(R_1,\dots,R_T\), the annualized Sharpe ratio
uses
\[
  \widehat{\SR}
  =
  \frac{12\,\bar R}{\sqrt{12}\,\widehat\sigma_R}.
\]
The Sortino ratio replaces \(\widehat\sigma_R\) with downside semideviation around the
zero-return threshold \citep{sortino_price1994}; drawdown-based and distribution-based
complements \citep{magdon_ismail_atiya2004,keating_shadwick2002} appear in the artifact
ledgers. Sharpe point estimates on 60 monthly observations carry wide sampling bands
\citep{lo2002sharpe}, which is why Section~\ref{sec:short_robustness} reports interval
and difference-test evidence rather than point estimates alone. These ratios are
convenient summaries, but they are not sufficient for a positive verdict. Any realized or
reported validation path that reaches zero wealth, breaches margin or collateral, or
requires an infeasible integer execution fails the survival gate regardless of Sharpe.
This rule directly addresses option-like return smoothing and rare crash losses
\citep{goetzmann_ingersoll_spiegel_welch2007}.

R1 baseline comparisons use paired circular-block bootstrap draws of the same calendar
months. The reported differences are monthly log growth and 95 percent expected shortfall
against the matched capped-naive option book and stock Markowitz. This keeps compounding
and tail loss central rather than testing only differences in arithmetic Sharpe.

Deployability is a separate requirement. R1 does not require one NAV of gross premium;
it reports the exposure that survives the cap, cost, and risk policy and holds the balance
in cash. A small deployed book can be a valid defensive decision but cannot be described
as evidence for a scalable high-return strategy.

The same logic applies to baseline dominance. A row that is positive but tied with capped
naive options does not validate the full theory. It may show that option exposure has a
premium, but it does not show that Greek-induced covariance, structural means, and conic
constraints improve on simple option diversification. The broad no-VIX row falls into
this category.

\subsection{Artifact discipline}

The empirical claim is backed by generated artifacts rather than hand-entered numbers.
The headline figures and tables are rebuilt from machine-readable robustness and cost
artifacts. The spread-source coverage table is built from the cost ledger. The validation
distributions are built from CPCV, Monte Carlo, refit, and rolling OOS outputs. The paper
verifier checks page quality, scope statements, artifact schemas, spread-source
policy, VIX settlement handling, and cap point-in-time behavior.

This discipline matters because three design errors identified during the research
process would otherwise be invisible in a summary table: charging default spreads to
names with observable quotes, penalizing capacity after optimization instead of
constraining it before, and a split-leg liquidity formulation that wastes displayed
capacity as pseudo-cash. Legacy E1 corrected the first two but retained a gross-equality
split representation and omitted costs from its headline objective. R1 corrects all three
and versions its artifacts separately so the development history is not erased.

The artifact ledger also protects against a common failure in option research:
inadvertently mixing data vintages. Liquidity caps use training-window volume only.
Fold-specific models rebuild the contract set and risk estimates from the fold's training
window. Broad spread proxy rows are point-in-time calibrated rather than filled from
future quote observations. These controls do not make the backtest live trading evidence,
but they keep the simulation internally auditable.

\subsection{Mechanism: breadth, mean regularization, and caps}

The result is not simply that more names improve the opportunity set. Breadth helps only
after the estimator and liquidity formulation are fixed together. The dominant estimator
mistake was the historical mean. In the larger+VIX universe, dropping it and using
structural-only forecasts recovers much of the gross Sharpe that the broad paper
configuration lost. Diagonal residual covariance and \(N\)-scaled shrinkage then remove a
second overfitting channel. The development-selected combination reaches a gross Sharpe of
\(2.010\) for the broad VIX-enabled book and produces the net Sharpe \(1.628\) after the
corrected cost stack.

Section~\ref{sec:short_robustness}. Second, the ablation does not claim channel-level
attribution of realized P\&L; it measures how the selected book changes when the
optimizer stops seeing one premium source.

The liquidity cap matters for a different reason. A post-hoc capacity penalty lets the
optimizer select positions first and then punishes them after the fact. A hard pre-trade
cap changes the feasible set before selection. The net-cap formulation also fixed a real
solver pathology: when caps were applied to split long/short legs, the solver could burn
offsetting exposure as pseudo-cash and consume good contracts' capacity. Binding
\(|w_i|\) after netting removed that artifact and left non-degenerate cases unchanged.

The VIX result is economically plausible. Equity options provide single-name convexity,
skew, and carry exposure. VIX options add a traded volatility instrument
\citep{whaley1993} that can respond directly to market-wide volatility jumps. The empirical result says that this extra state
channel matters in the tested sample. It does not say that VIX options are free hedges:
they have their own spreads, settlement requirements, and wing-liquidity caveats, and
the paper keeps those rows marked as proxied where matched historical CBBO is absent.

The cap result is equally important. The old optimizer could generate attractive gross
allocations that were later destroyed by capacity costs. The new formulation moves the
constraint into the decision. That changes the selected book, not just the accounting
afterward. At \(\$1\) million NAV and \(X=0.05\), the optimizer chooses a smaller,
cleaner set of trades that the cap vector can support. At larger NAV, the cap vector
itself becomes too small to support full deployment, so the correct conclusion is not to
increase leverage but to stop promoting the book.

\subsection{Interpretation by scenario}

The \texttt{orig} no-VIX row is useful mainly as a control. It uses exact historical
CBBO for the eight equity names and achieves a positive point estimate, but it is
capacity-infeasible under the hard cap budget. The result therefore validates pieces of
the framework without becoming a deployable headline book.

The \texttt{orig+VIX} row is a stronger result. It uses exact
equity-option CBBO for the eight names and an inferred proxy for the VIX spread rows. It
beats stock Markowitz and capped naive options, and its MC refit p05 remains above one
Sharpe point. The caveat is execution data for VIX rows, not the basic allocation
mechanism.

The \texttt{larger} no-VIX row is mixed. The regularization and cap fixes make the result
positive, but the optimizer does not beat capped naive options by a meaningful amount and
it remains below the stock Markowitz Sharpe. This row is evidence that breadth alone is
not enough.

The \texttt{larger+VIX} row is the strongest result. It has the best E1 Sharpe, best
Sortino, strongest rolling Sharpe, and strongest refit-stability statistics. It also
carries the largest spread-imputation dependence, because roughly four-fifths of broad
cost rows use the inferred CBBO proxy rather than exact historical CBBO; its performance
should be read under the calibrated spread assumptions of
Section~\ref{sec:short_application}.

\subsection{Economic interpretation}

The four scenarios separate three mechanisms. The first is option structure: E1 has to
beat capped naive options, otherwise the theory adds little. The second is volatility
instrument access: adding VIX materially improves both the original and broad universes.
The third is breadth: adding names helps only when the estimator is regularized and VIX is
present. Without VIX, the broad universe becomes positive but does not clear the baseline
hurdle.

The result also shows why option-only strategies should be evaluated by deployable
notional rather than by nominal contract count. The broad universe contains many more
contracts, but the added liquidity budget is much smaller than the added choice set.
That asymmetry creates the central estimation-capacity tradeoff. More names reduce
idiosyncratic dependence and create more spread opportunities, but they also increase
mean-estimation noise and may not increase executable premium capacity enough.

The strongest practical lesson is that breadth and liquidity caps are complements. A
breadth expansion without cap discipline lets the optimizer choose thin options and later
pay capacity penalties. A cap without mean regularization can keep trades small but still
select noisy expected returns. The positive VIX-enabled rows appear only when both pieces
are present.

\subsection{What the point estimates do and do not say}

The point estimates are economically meaningful because they are full-cost net numbers,
but they remain sample estimates. A net Sharpe of \(1.628\) for \texttt{larger+VIX} does
not mean the population Sharpe is known to three decimals. It means that, in the realized
development path and under the corrected ex-post cost stack, the legacy E1 book earned a substantially
better risk-adjusted return than both baselines. The validation section then asks whether
that ordering survives alternate histories and refits, and
Table~\ref{tab:short_inference_panel} attaches confidence intervals, deflated Sharpe
ratios, and formal Sharpe-difference tests to these point estimates.

The large Sortino ratios should be read similarly. Option books can have asymmetric
return distributions, so downside semideviation is a useful complement to standard
deviation. But Sortino ratios can also look high when the sample has few downside months.
That is why the paper does not rely on Sortino alone. It reports rolling OOS, MC lower
tails, drawdown breach rates, and CPCV stress tests in the same result package.

The stock Markowitz baseline is not a perfect substitute for the option book because it
lacks convexity and volatility exposure. It is nevertheless a hard economic comparison:
if an option strategy cannot beat a simple stock portfolio after paying option trading
costs, the option-specific complexity is not justified. The capped naive option baseline
is the complementary comparison: it keeps the option asset class but removes the theory.
Passing both baselines is why the VIX-enabled rows deserve attention.

The broad no-VIX row is the clearest example of why this distinction matters. It has a
positive Sharpe, so it is not a failed strategy in the narrow sense. But it is almost
identical to capped naive options and below the stock baseline. A paper that promoted
that row as evidence for Greek Markowitz would overstate the finding. Here it is marked
mixed and used to show that breadth without a traded volatility instrument is not enough
in the current sample.

\section{Robustness and distributional checks}\label{sec:short_robustness}

\subsection{Cross-validation and overfitting controls}

The validation layer first asks whether the result depends too much on one chronological
split. Combinatorially purged cross-validation is run in two designs. The
\emph{liquid-era} design divides the post-2018 monthly panel into 12 contiguous groups;
each fold refits the legacy E1 procedure on the remaining groups after a one-month purge
and embargo, scores the held-out pair, and the \(\binom{12}{2}=66\) splits recombine into
11 complete backtest paths. The \emph{claim-window} design applies the identical machinery
but partitions only the post-2020 evaluation months on which every claim in this paper is
made, training each fold on all earlier history plus the non-held-out claim months. The
liquid-era restriction is deliberate, and the next paragraphs justify it directly from the
data: recombining folds across the entire 2015--2026 panel fails for every book, but the
failure is concentrated in 2015--2017 months when the listed-options panel was too thin
for the modern per-contract cost model to be realistic, not in the COVID period. The PBO
summary combines the 12 blocked splits and 66 CPCV splits, for 78 total CV splits.

CPCV of either design is a robustness diagnostic, not a tradable out-of-sample history.
Some CPCV training sets include months after the test months, by construction. Its role
is to stress the selection and path distribution, estimate probability of backtest
overfitting, and reveal whether a result comes from one lucky chronological window. The
paper therefore reports CPCV and PBO as validation evidence, while the trading claim
remains tied to legacy E1 and the static/rolling internal-validation protocols.

early listed-options panel, producing isolated months with modeled net returns below

the pre-2018 months flips every book positive.\footnote{The full 2015--2026 CPCV ledger is
superseded in the current artifact set by the liquid-era design; the full-sample and
ex-2020 quantiles quoted here trace to the archived pre-restriction path ledger in the
repository history.} One genuine tail does survive the
restriction. The VIX-enabled books still carry a single March-2020 volatility-spike month
whose modeled loss exceeds a month of premium, and because CPCV recombination places that
month in every path, the compounded VIX wealth paths are absorbed at zero (Default share
\(1.0\)) even though the return-based path Sharpe stays positive; that is a disclosed
short-volatility tail, not a data artifact. Under R1's hard survival gate, this evidence
forces a failed verdict for the legacy VIX candidates; positive Sharpe cannot override
economic insolvency. The claim-window design, on the pure post-2020
period, confirms the reading, with fifth-percentile and median path Sharpes of
\(0.44/0.49\) for \texttt{orig}, \(1.00/1.06\) for \texttt{orig+VIX}, \(0.66/0.71\) for
\texttt{larger}, and \(1.45/1.47\) for \texttt{larger+VIX}.

The relative statistic aligns with the paper's verdicts. Because the naive baselines are
scored on the same folds under the same costs, per-path Sharpe differences between E1 and
the matched capped-naive book difference out the common cost drag. On the liquid-era
folds, E1 beats its matched naive book at the fifth percentile for the two VIX-enabled
universes (\(+0.21\) and \(+0.10\)) but not for the two no-VIX universes (\(-0.56\) and
\(-0.27\)); the claim-window relative paths separate the same way, more sharply, at
\(+2.26\) and \(+1.50\) for the VIX books and \(-0.13\) and \(-0.15\) for the no-VIX books.
The no-VIX shortfall is worth stating carefully: the locked static \texttt{orig} book does
beat its naive baseline (Table~\ref{tab:short_inference_panel}), but that advantage is not
stable under fold-level refitting, which is the CPCV analogue of the mixed and
capacity-diagnostic verdicts those rows already carry.

Two features of this analysis bear emphasis. First, the pass/mixed verdicts reported in
Section~\ref{sec:short_results} use the liquid-era CPCV tail as one of five robustness
criteria, but a book that clears all five robustness screens without beating its
capped-naive baseline is still reported as mixed rather than promoted; this is why the
broad no-VIX book remains mixed even though its liquid-era CPCV is positive. Second, the
full 2015--2026 result is not quietly dropped because it fails: the failure, and its
mechanism---the modern cost stack applied to a pre-2018 thin panel, with 2020 shown not to
be the cause---are reported here directly, so restricting the long-history CPCV to the
liquid era is a disclosed data-quality decision rather than a search for a friendlier
statistic.

Traditional Markowitz on the broad universe is a revealing baseline under the same
screens. A zero-cost 56-equity tangency portfolio---the textbook plug-in Markowitz rule,
given every advantage by charging it no trading cost---earns only \(0.115\) net Sharpe over
the test window, and its recombined and resampled distributions are weak throughout:
liquid-era CPCV fifth percentile \(-0.18\), claim-window \(-0.34\), Monte Carlo resampled
\(-0.46\), and Monte Carlo refit \(-0.64\). The estimation error that an eight-name stock
Markowitz book largely avoids (Sharpe \(0.91\)) overwhelms the plug-in rule at 56 names,
which is exactly the breadth-versus-estimation tension the option framework answers with
structural means and regularized covariance. The broad \texttt{larger+VIX} E1 book beats
this broad stock Markowitz baseline on every one of these screens (Figures~%
\ref{fig:short_scoreboard} and \ref{fig:short_distributions}), and the broad no-VIX
\texttt{larger} book, though it only ties its capped-naive option baseline, still clears the
broad stock baseline comfortably.

The PBO interpretation is similarly bounded. The PBO rank screen asks whether the row
chosen in sample tends to rank poorly out of sample across the cross-validation splits.
Passing this screen reduces concern that the observed row is merely a backtest winner
among many alternatives. It does not eliminate development risk, because the research
program itself learned from earlier failed configurations. The paper handles that by
locking E1 before the final robustness run and by reporting the no-VIX and proxy-spread
caveats plainly.

\subsection{Monte Carlo, repricing, and path risk}

The Monte Carlo layer has three roles. Resampled historical universes preserve the joint
cross-sectional strategy-return dependence while changing month order through
circular-block sampling. Refit stability resamples training histories, refits the locked
procedure, and measures how sensitive the final book is to training-sample variation.
Repriced synthetic universes rebuild option paths from simulated state variables and
model prices, then apply a documented historical-cost overlay.

The repriced layer is a stress world by construction. It is not synthetic NBBO.
Black--Scholes and Black--76 repricing can test exposure to spot, volatility, VIX, and
state-path assumptions, but it does not reproduce historical order books, queue priority,
displayed size, or market-maker widening. Repriced synthetic paths therefore answer a
model-risk question, not an execution-quality question. Tail-path simulation diagnostics
are path-risk diagnostics only.

The failure or mixed status of repriced overlay rows should not be over-read. A repriced
Black--Scholes/Black--76 world does not contain the same historical option risk premia as
the observed market. If the strategy earns compensation from selling or buying specific
real-world insurance premia, a synthetic repricing model can remove part of the edge by
construction. The useful information is sensitivity: which variants remain stable when
the option surface is rebuilt from state paths, and which variants depend heavily on the
observed realized surface.

Drawdown breach simulations and GARCH/EGARCH-style volatility diagnostics then ask a
different question: how likely is the strategy to violate a risk tolerance under stressed
path dynamics? EGARCH is used when the sample is long enough; otherwise the implementation
falls back to the simpler volatility-path diagnostic. The paper does not promote those
simulations to alternate realized histories. They are scenario tools for risk review.

\subsection{Multiple testing and rolling OOS}

The multiple-testing layer prevents the paper from treating the best observed row as a
free discovery. The reality-check and deflated-Sharpe style diagnostics
\citep{white2000reality,hansen2005spa,bailey_lopezdeprado2014} account for strategy
search and finite-sample selection; HAC-based family inference \citep{neweywest1987}
stays in the artifact ledgers. The legacy E1 robustness run reduces fold-level retuning but not this
caveat: the final validation does not re-tune the specification inside test folds. Nevertheless, the
model was developed through earlier diagnostics, so the paper reports the remaining
selection risk rather than claiming a pristine randomized experiment.

The Sharpe-difference tests \citep{jobson_korkie1981,memmel2003}, computed on paired
monthly series and confirmed by a paired circular-block bootstrap in the spirit of
\citet{ledoit_wolf2008sharpe}, draw the sharpest distinction in the paper. Against the
capped-naive baselines, the optimizer edge of the VIX-enabled books is large and
statistically significant: \(+2.273\) Sharpe points for \texttt{orig+VIX} and \(+1.335\)
for \texttt{larger+VIX}, both with \(p<0.001\), and \(+0.413\) with \(p=0.014\) even for
the capacity-infeasible \texttt{orig} diagnostic. The \texttt{larger} no-VIX edge over
naive is essentially zero and slightly negative (\(-0.009\), \(p=0.967\)), which is the
formal version of the ``mixed'' verdict. Against stock Markowitz, however, no variant's
Sharpe advantage is statistically significant on 60 months: the differences range from
\(-0.322\) to \(+0.719\) with p-values between \(0.179\) and \(0.776\).

This asymmetry belongs in the statement of results, not in a footnote. The finding that
survives formal testing is that Greek-induced allocation with VIX access beats naive
option diversification after full costs. The finding that the VIX-enabled books beat the
stock baseline is a point-estimate ordering that the current sample length cannot
separate statistically; a \(0.72\) annualized Sharpe gap simply requires more than 60
monthly observations to resolve at conventional levels. The paper therefore leans on the
baseline comparisons, the rolling OOS refits, and the MC refit distributions jointly
rather than on any single test.

Rolling 36-month OOS refits are the most trading-like validation in the current package.
They refit the model through time and score subsequent months under the same cost and
liquidity rules. The rolling Sharpe values are \(1.273\) for \texttt{larger+VIX}, \(1.033\)
for \texttt{orig+VIX}, \(0.585\) for \texttt{orig}, and \(0.480\) for \texttt{larger}.
Those numbers reinforce the ordering in the static scoreboard: VIX-enabled E1 performs;
no-VIX breadth is not enough.

Figure~\ref{fig:short_walk_forward_paths} is the most useful visual bridge from the
scoreboard to the validation logic. It shows that the VIX-enabled E1 rows do not win only
through one terminal statistic; their cumulative paths recover from the early sample
drawdown and then separate after 2024. It also shows why the capped-naive VIX baselines
are not acceptable substitutes: the matched naive VIX books decay sharply even though
some naive no-VIX paths recover later. The regular stock Markowitz path is a strong
baseline, but it does not match the rolling OOS compounding of the two VIX-enabled E1
variants.

\subsection{Interpreting the robustness tests}

The tests are not averaged into a single meta-score because they answer different
questions. Capacity asks whether the target book can be deployed at the specified NAV.
Baseline dominance asks whether the option theory beats simpler alternatives.
Liquid-era CPCV asks whether path ordering and fold composition create fragility beyond
the claimed period within the liquid panel; claim-window CPCV asks the same question
inside it. MC
resampling asks whether
the realized sequence is unusually favorable. MC refit asks whether the procedure is
stable to training histories. Repriced overlay asks whether the result depends on the
realized option surface and cost path. Rolling OOS asks whether the model can be refit
through time without relying on one static split.

The pass/mixed/fail labels should therefore be read qualitatively. A repriced no-premium
overlay failure does not automatically reject the static candidate, because that design is
a deliberately adversarial cost-and-surface stress rather than a trading protocol. A
capacity failure does reject deployable status,
because a book that cannot be formed at the target NAV is not a trading candidate. A
repriced overlay failure is a model-risk warning, not proof that historical edge is
illusory. The paper's final ranking follows the tests that are closest to the intended
claim: deployability, full-cost baseline dominance, claim-window CPCV, MC refit
stability, rolling OOS, and spread-source transparency.

This framework is also why the paper does not chase a perfect all-green heatmap. A
stress battery worth reporting should contain visible failures. If every robustness
test were passed easily, the likely explanation would be that the tests are too weak.
The goal is to identify which limitations remain after the main methodological errors
have been fixed.

\section{Capacity, execution, and limitations}\label{sec:short_constraints}

\subsection{Capacity and scale}

The main practical brake is market depth. At \(\$1\) million NAV, the legacy VIX-enabled
books passed E1's capacity screen but fail R1's survival interpretation because of the
absorbed-zero paths. Above roughly \(\$2\) million, the
representative month-end option contracts no longer support full deployment under the
tested participation caps. At \(\$5\) million and above, the sum of train-window liquidity
caps falls below one NAV for every tested configuration and participation level. Positive
Sharpe rows that deploy only a tiny fraction of NAV are economically vacuous and are not
promoted.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/short_capacity_spread_panel.pdf}

\end{figure}

Breadth increases the cap budget, but not in proportion to the number of underlyings.
Moving from eight equity names to 56 names increases the number of candidate contracts by
far more than it increases executable month-end premium capacity. Many added-name options
are thin. The option market, not the optimizer, becomes the wall; this is a capacity
diagnostic rather than a positive trading claim.

This finding also determines how the capacity result should be read for implementation.
Sizing should not begin by asking how much capital can be allocated to the best Sharpe
row; it should ask what NAV can be deployed while respecting per-contract displayed
size, per-underlying concentration, and margin limits. The research answer points to a
small-account allocation, not a scalable standalone fund. Additional capital is better
spent expanding the execution universe with verified quotes and displayed size than
multiplying the same representative contracts.

\subsection{Execution caveat}

The spread proxy is the largest remaining empirical caveat. It is a major improvement over
the old default spread assumptions and stale quote snapshots, but it still cannot replace
matched market-hours historical NBBO/CBBO for every broad and VIX row. The correct use of
the broad results is therefore calibrated execution sensitivity: if broad large-cap and
VIX spreads behave like the liquid CBBO surface used for imputation, legacy E1 has positive arithmetic results
with VIX at \(\$1\) million NAV. The result should not be read as a proof that every
historical target contract could have been filled at the imputed quote.

The same distinction applies to margin and assignment. The simulation charges margin
drag and assignment penalties, but a simulated penalty is not a broker statement, so
execution quality at realized fills is not claimed; establishing it would require
market-hours quotes with displayed size, realized fill records, broker margin, and
reconciliation against actual statements, all outside the scope of this paper.

Ex-dividend and settlement handling is especially important for options. American
single-name options can be assigned early, especially around ex-dividend dates and deep
in-the-money short calls. VIX options have their own settlement mechanics and require
exact VRO/SOQ handling for expiry P\&L. These are not cosmetic accounting details. They
can change realized cashflows and margin usage. The simulation therefore prices these
events with explicit penalties and settlement labels, and the verification harness
requires exact settlement data before VIX rows enter the results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:short_conclusion}

The paper does not establish a new mathematical theory of option portfolios. Premium
accounting, Greek Taylor maps, covariance transformation, mean--variance utility, CVaR,
and convex constraints are established tools. The repair makes their relationship
internally correct: a Greek loading matrix requires factor/residual cross covariance;
predictable costs belong in the allocation objective; a utility objective chooses scale;
and gross premium is an upper bound that permits cash.

The empirical contribution is correspondingly narrower. R1 is an auditable monthly
procedure with point-in-time costs, capacity, margin, collateral, assignment screening,
integer execution, and a hard survival gate. The previously reported E1 Sharpes remain
useful development history, but E1 was selected after inspection of the post-2020 window
and its VIX books have absorbed-zero CPCV wealth paths. Those facts prevent a confirmatory
or survival claim regardless of the positive arithmetic Sharpe estimates.

All currently available observations are therefore labeled retrospective development
data. A machine-readable freeze manifest records the R1 source hashes, risk policy, data
cutoff, and first eligible future decision date. No confirmatory claim is permitted until
36 untouched monthly observations have accumulated. Exact broad and VIX executable quote
history, broker margin and assignment records, routed fills, and longer prospective data
remain necessary before the procedure can be described as a tradable strategy.
